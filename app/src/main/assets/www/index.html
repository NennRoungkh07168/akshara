Where the scripts come from

From Brahmi to Khmer

  1. 3rd century BCE

    Brahmi appears in the edicts of Emperor Ashoka across India. Nearly every script of South and Southeast Asia descends from it.

  2. c. 4th–9th century CE

    Southern Brahmi forms develop in South India, including the scripts of the Pallava period at Kanchipuram and Mamallapuram, some of them highly ornate.

  3. c. 5th century CE

    Sanskrit inscriptions in Indic letterforms appear in Southeast Asia, such as the yūpa (sacrificial post) inscriptions of Kutai in Borneo.

  4. 611 CE

    An inscription from Angkor Borei (catalogued K.600) is the earliest dated text containing the Khmer language.

  5. 9th–14th century

    Old Khmer flourishes in the Angkor period, carved in temple stone alongside Sanskrit.

  6. Today

    Modern Khmer script, encoded in Unicode since 1999, is written by about 17 million speakers.

The other four scripts

Aa

Latin (German, English)

Phoenician letters passed to the Greeks, then the Etruscans and Romans. German was widely printed in Fraktur blackletter until 1941, and adds ä, ö, ü and ß.

ع

Arabic

Grew out of the Nabataean form of Aramaic script around the 4th–6th centuries CE. Written right to left, with 28 letters that change shape as they join.

字

Chinese

Traceable to Shang dynasty oracle-bone inscriptions, around 1250 BCE. Characters stand for words and syllables, not single sounds. Simplified forms were standardized in mainland China in the 1950s–60s.

Pyu

Used by the Pyu city-states of Myanmar (Sri Ksetra, Halin, Beikthano) in the first millennium CE, in a Brahmic script. Much of what we know comes from the Myazedi inscription of 1113 CE, which repeats one text in Pali, Mon, Old Burmese and Pyu.

𓂀

Egyptian and Greek, for comparison

Egyptian hieroglyphs date from about 3200 BCE; the Pyramid Texts inside Old Kingdom pyramids are Egyptian religious spells, not Greek mythology. The Greek alphabet came from Phoenician around the 8th century BCE. Visual similarity between symbols in different scripts doesn't prove a historical link.

Ancient Egypt

Language, signs, grammar, pyramids, the Pyramid Texts, art and sculpture, with tools to write your own name and numbers.

The Egyptian language

Egyptian is an Afroasiatic language, a distant cousin of Arabic, Hebrew and Berber, and it has one of the longest written histories of any language: about 4,500 years.

  1. c. 3200 BCE

    First hieroglyphs: short labels and names on tags, pots and palettes at Abydos.

  2. c. 2600–2000 BCE · Old Egyptian

    The language of the pyramid age and of the Pyramid Texts.

  3. c. 2000–1300 BCE · Middle Egyptian

    The classical stage, used for literature and monuments for centuries afterwards. Most students learn this first.

  4. c. 1300–700 BCE · Late Egyptian

    The spoken language of the New Kingdom, with new grammar such as articles ("the", "a").

  5. c. 650 BCE–450 CE · Demotic

    A later stage of the language, written in a very fast cursive script.

  6. from c. 3rd century CE · Coptic

    Egyptian written in the Greek alphabet plus six or seven letters taken from Demotic. It faded as an everyday language after the Arabic conquest and is still used in Coptic Christian worship.

Four ways of writing one language

𓅓

Hieroglyphic

Pictorial signs for monuments, carved or painted. Written right to left, left to right, or in columns. You read toward the faces: if the birds look left, start on the left.

Hieratic

A joined-up handwriting version of hieroglyphs, written with a reed brush on papyrus, used from the earliest periods for letters, accounts and literature.

Demotic

An even more abbreviated script from about 650 BCE, used for daily business. It is the middle script on the Rosetta Stone.

Ⲁ

Coptic

Greek letters plus extra ones, and the first Egyptian writing to show vowels. Much of our knowledge of how Egyptian sounded comes from it.

How it was deciphered: the Greek connection

After Alexander the Great (332 BCE), Greek became an official language in Egypt. The Rosetta Stone, a decree of Ptolemy V from 196 BCE, repeats one text in hieroglyphic, Demotic and Greek. It was found in 1799, and using it with other bilingual texts and his knowledge of Coptic, Jean-François Champollion announced the decipherment in 1822. He started from royal names in cartouches, such as Ptolemy and Cleopatra, which are spelled with sound signs.

The signs

Classical Egyptian used roughly 700–1,000 signs, catalogued by Alan Gardiner into lettered groups: A for men, B women, D body parts, G birds, M plants, N sky and earth, and so on (code G17 is the owl). There are three kinds of sign:

  • Sound signs (phonograms) for one, two or three consonants.
  • Word signs (logograms), often with a stroke 𓏤 meaning "this sign means what it shows".
  • Determinatives, silent signs at the end of a word that show its category.

Vowels were not written. Egyptologists pronounce words by placing an "e" between consonants: nfr becomes "nefer", ḥtp "hetep". The real ancient vowels are partly known from Coptic and from foreign spellings.

Grammar in brief

Word order

Verb first, then subject, then object: "hears the man the words". Adjectives follow nouns: pr nfr, "house good" = a good house.

Gender and number

Feminine words end in -t, the bread loaf 𓏏: sꜣ son, sꜣt daughter. Plurals end in -w (masculine) or -wt (feminine), and are often shown with three strokes 𓏥. Egyptian also had a dual for pairs.

Verbs

The basic form is the sḏm.f, "he hears", built by adding a suffix pronoun to the verb. Adding .n makes the past: sḏm.n.f, "he heard". Egyptian grammar is about aspect and emphasis as much as time.

"Of"

Possession is shown by putting nouns side by side, pr nswt, "house of the king", or with the word n(j) 𓈖, "of".

Honour before order

Words for gods and kings are written first out of respect, even when they're read later. Ḥm-nṯr, "servant of the god" (a priest), is written with 𓊹 "god" before 𓍛 "servant".

The pyramids

  1. before c. 2700 BCE

    Kings and nobles are buried under mastabas, flat-topped mud-brick tombs.

  2. c. 2650 BCE · Djoser

    The Step Pyramid at Saqqara, designed by Imhotep: six mastabas stacked in stone, the first large stone building in the world.

  3. c. 2600 BCE · Sneferu

    Experiments toward a true pyramid: Meidum, the Bent Pyramid (its angle changes halfway up), then the Red Pyramid at Dahshur, the first successful smooth-sided one.

  4. c. 2560–2500 BCE · Giza

    Khufu's Great Pyramid, about 146 m high when complete, then Khafre's (with the Great Sphinx, generally attributed to him) and Menkaure's. Their insides are bare: no texts.

  5. 5th Dynasty

    Smaller pyramids at Abusir and Saqqara, with more effort spent on temples, relief carving and sun temples for Ra.

  6. c. 2350 BCE · Unas

    The first pyramid with texts carved inside: the Pyramid Texts.

  7. 6th Dynasty

    Teti, Pepi I, Merenre and Pepi II continue the texts, and some queens' pyramids have them too.

  8. Middle Kingdom and later

    Mud-brick pyramids with stone casing. The spells move to coffins (Coffin Texts) and then to papyrus scrolls (the Book of the Dead).

A pyramid was a complex

A valley temple at the edge of the floodplain, a covered causeway, a mortuary temple against the pyramid's east face for daily offerings, the pyramid itself, a small satellite pyramid, and often pits for full-size boats. The pyramid's shape probably echoes the benben, the first mound of creation, and the slanting rays of the sun. The hieroglyph 𓉴 is the word mr, pyramid.

Inside Unas's pyramid

A sloping corridor leads to an antechamber and a burial chamber holding the sarcophagus. Their walls are covered in columns of texts filled with blue-green pigment, and the ceilings are carved with stars, so the king rests under a night sky.

The Pyramid Texts

The oldest large collection of religious writing in the world. Kurt Sethe's edition (1908–1922) numbered 714 "utterances", and more have been found since. They were recited as rituals and carved so they would work forever. These are my own simplified renderings, not quotations from published translations.

Spell 213: "You have not gone away dead"

ḏd mdw: hꜣ Wnjs, n šm.n.k js mt.t(j), šm.n.k ꜥnḫ.t(j)

Words to be spoken: O Unas, you did not go away dead; you went away alive. Sit on the throne of Osiris, sceptre in hand, and give orders to the living.

The opening of the burial-chamber sequence in Unas's pyramid: death is recast as a departure to new life.

Spell 217: Announcing the king to the sun god

O Ra-Atum, this Unas comes to you, a spirit that cannot be destroyed. Your son comes to you.

Messengers are sent to the four quarters of the world, to Seth and Nephthys, Osiris and Isis, and others, to announce that the king is coming to the sky.

Spells 273–274: The "Cannibal Hymn"

The sky darkens, the stars tremble, the bones of the earth gods shake when they see Unas rise as a power. He feeds on the gods, and takes in their magic and their spirits.

A vivid image, not a literal meal: by "eating" the gods the king absorbs their powers and becomes the greatest of them.

Offering spells

Osiris Unas, take the Eye of Horus. It is your bread, your beer, your water.

Each food, drink, oil and cloth in the funeral ritual is named as the Eye of Horus, which Horus lost fighting Seth and which was healed. Giving it brings the king back to wholeness.

Ascension spells

A ladder is set up for the king; he rises as a falcon, as a goose, on the smoke of incense; he joins the circumpolar stars, the "imperishable ones" that never set.

Serpent spells

Short, powerful protective charms against snakes and dangerous creatures, some written in an older dialect or a Semitic language that scholars are still working on.

Spell 600: Creation and protection

O Atum-Khepri, you rose up as the hill, you shone as the benben stone in Heliopolis. You sneezed out Shu and spat out Tefnut. Put your arms around this king and his pyramid, so that his life-force is in it forever.

A 6th-dynasty spell that ties the pyramid's shape to the first hill of creation.

Art: the rules

The composite view

A figure shows each part from its most recognisable angle: head, legs and feet in profile, eye and shoulders from the front, chest turned. It is a complete description, not a snapshot.

Size means importance

Gods and kings are largest, then officials, then servants. A wife may be shown knee-high beside her husband as a sign of relationship, not actual height.

The grid

Artists drew on a grid. In the Old and Middle Kingdoms a standing figure measured 18 squares from hairline to soles; from about the 7th century BCE a 21-square grid was used. This is why figures look alike across centuries.

Registers

Scenes are stacked in horizontal bands, each on its own ground line, read like lines of a comic.

Men were usually painted reddish-brown and women paler yellow: a convention for outdoor and indoor life, not a measurement of skin.

Words and pictures are one system

Hieroglyphs are tiny pictures and pictures follow hieroglyphic rules. A large figure can be the determinative of the name beside it, and signs face the same way as the figure they belong to.

The exception: Amarna

Under Akhenaten (about 1350 BCE) the rules loosened: elongated faces, rounded bellies, intimate family scenes. After him, the traditional style returned.

Sculpture

Statues were not portraits to admire but homes for the ka, the life-force, which could live in the image and receive offerings. Djoser's seated statue was sealed in a serdab, a closed room with eye-holes, so his ka could look out at the rituals.

Common types

  • Seated king: hands on knees, looking straight ahead. Khafre's statue in hard gneiss has the falcon god Horus wrapping its wings around his head.
  • Striding figure: left foot forward, arms by the sides, fists closed. The pose lasted almost 3,000 years and influenced early Greek kouros statues.
  • Pair statues: Menkaure and his queen, her arm around his waist.
  • Triads: Menkaure with the goddess Hathor and a region's god.
  • Seated scribe: cross-legged, papyrus on his lap, with inlaid crystal eyes (Louvre, Paris).
  • Reserve heads: plain limestone heads placed in Giza tombs, purpose still debated.
  • Block statues: from the Middle Kingdom, a man seated with knees drawn up inside a cloak, a perfect surface for texts.
  • Sphinx: a lion's body with the king's head, power plus intelligence.

How they were made

Stone was dressed with copper chisels, harder stone pounders (dolerite) and sand abrasive. Sculptors drew the grid on each face of the block and cut inward, which keeps the figure frontal. Hard-stone statues usually keep a back pillar and the stone between arms and body, for strength and permanence. Finished statues were painted, and eyes were sometimes inlaid with crystal and stone.

Relief

Raised relief, where the background is cut away, was used indoors; sunk relief, where the figure is cut into the surface, caught strong sunlight outdoors. The Pyramid Texts in your photos are sunk hieroglyphs filled with paint.

Roadmap

What exists now, and what turns this prototype into a full offline language school.

1. PrototypeBuilt

  • Phrase exchange across any pair of five languages, with practice mode and saved progress.
  • Khmer alphabet with series, digits and Indic sound origins.
  • Brahmi → Pallava → Pyu → Grantha → Khmer → Thai comparison, with a slot for your own Pallava images.
  • Language family trees, Pyu, and a Pyramid Texts reader with your photos.
  • Full transcriptions and translations of all eight uploaded images, a heritage vocabulary in five languages, and the interface in five languages.
  • Research core: a text analyser with Leipzig-style interlinear glosses for Egyptian, Sanskrit, Tamil, Old Khmer and the five languages, with a grammar comparison table; four hand-checked analysed readings.
  • Pallava writer: the Akshara Pallava font (all 33 consonants, traced from the Aksharamukha Pallava alphabet), Grantha Unicode encoding, transliteration input, on-screen keyboard, copy and font download.
  • A camera scanner: Claude reads photos of writing in any of the app's scripts and translates them into all five languages, plus offline compare-by-eye charts and a scan history.
  • An Egypt section: language history, 70+ signs, quiz, grammar, name and number writer, pyramids, Pyramid Texts, art and sculpture.

2. Verify the contentNext

  • Native speakers review every phrase, and the interface translations, in German, Arabic, Chinese and Khmer.
  • Translate the longer explanations into all five languages once the English is final.
  • Pallava vowel signs, virāma forms, conjuncts and numerals: needs a documented source table or inscription photos, then they go into the font with proper shaping rules.
  • Have an Egyptologist review the Egypt section, and add full spell texts with hieroglyphs and line-by-line translations.
  • Check each Pallava drawing from the Wimonkasem table against DHARMA corpus photos and estampages, and record the inscription ID and date.
  • Ask the publishers for permission, or swap in openly licensed charts, before sharing the app publicly.

3. Real voices

  • Record native-speaker audio. Many phones have no Khmer text-to-speech voice, so recordings matter most there.

4. True offline app

  • Package as an installable web app with a service worker, bundling the free Noto fonts (SIL Open Font License).
  • Then Android and iOS builds for app stores.

5. Deeper library

  • Levels from beginner to intermediate, grammar notes, reading practice.
  • A Pallava research font mapped to private-use codes, built from the verified glyph table.
  • Monument and sculpture entries using open-access museum images.
  • App interface translated into all five languages.

6. Language exchange community

  • Partner matching between learners, with privacy controls, moderation, and extra protections for young learners.
